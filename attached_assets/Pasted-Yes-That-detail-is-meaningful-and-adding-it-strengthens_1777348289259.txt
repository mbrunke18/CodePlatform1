The full revised post
Decision rights migrate quietly.
I watched it happen across twenty years inside Ford, Toyota, Lockheed Martin, Schwab, Vantiv, Boyd Gaming, and CDI. The migration was never announced. It was never put on an agenda. It happened one approval threshold at a time.
A new vendor onboarding workflow gets approved with a system-driven approval step instead of an executive sign off. Rational. Defensible. Speeds up procurement.
A capital reallocation under a certain dollar threshold gets routed through a finance dashboard instead of a CFO review. Rational. Defensible. Speeds up planning.
A policy update under a certain compliance threshold gets logged in the GRC platform without legal review. Rational. Defensible. Speeds up operations.
Each one of those decisions cleared its own business case. Each one looked like a small efficiency improvement in the room where it was approved. None of them were stopped. None of them were tracked together.
What happened over a decade was that the decision rights on paper no longer matched the decision rights in practice. The executive layer still owned authority by org chart. The systems owned authority by workflow. Nobody said that out loud because nobody was tracking the migration as a single phenomenon.
AI is now accelerating this pattern by an order of magnitude. Outputs become defaults. Defaults become actions. By the time someone asks who approved this, it is already done.
This is not an AI problem. This is an operating model problem that AI is making faster and more visible. The fix is not slowing AI down. The fix is putting the executive authorization step back into the architecture as a required threshold the system cannot cross without it.
I have spent the last three years building exactly that. Decision rights mapped before the trigger fires. Executive authorization required at the decision layer with four real options. Run the staged response. Audible to a different staged response. Customize the response in real time. Choose to do nothing. The architecture exists in production today, designed from the operational reality I watched fail across two decades.
The decision rights have to live somewhere. If the executive layer does not hold them, the system holds them by default. There is no neutral position.
