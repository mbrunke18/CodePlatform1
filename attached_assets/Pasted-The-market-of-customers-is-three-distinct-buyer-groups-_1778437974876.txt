The market of customers is three distinct buyer groups and each one hears category language differently. Let me give you the honest read on each.
Buyer Group One. Fortune 1000 C-Suite. CEO, COO, CIO, CFO, Chief Strategy Officer.
These are your primary economic buyers. The people who write the check for a $75,000 Founding Partner pilot.
What resonates with this group is outcome language not category language. They do not buy infrastructure. They buy fearlessness. They buy the ability to move at speed when something real fires. They buy the confidence that when the activist stake is disclosed or the ransomware hits or the M&A window opens the organization will not spend 30 days figuring out who owns the response.
Readiness Infrastructure will land with this group if it is immediately followed by what it produces. Not Readiness Infrastructure as a standalone category name. Readiness Infrastructure that makes your organization fearless in the face of any strategic trigger. The infrastructure framing is the credibility signal. The fearless outcome is the reason they buy.
The CIO who attended Evanta last year and sat through three sessions on AI operating model gaps will hear Readiness Infrastructure and immediately understand that you are describing something that sits above their Microsoft investment and below their organizational response capability. That gap is real to them. The category name fits the gap.
Buyer Group Two. Boards of Directors and Investors.
These are your validation and funding buyers. The people who approve the pilot budget or write the Series A check.
What resonates with this group is risk language and structural differentiation. Boards care about whether the organization is exposed. They care about whether the CEO has the infrastructure to respond when something material happens. They care about whether the company is prepared or improvising.
Readiness Infrastructure lands strongly with boards because it is a governance claim not a technology claim. You are not selling software. You are selling organizational readiness. That is a board-level concept. A board member who reads Readiness Infrastructure for Strategic Trigger Response understands immediately that this is the preparation layer their organization either has or does not have. The absence of it is a governance risk. The presence of it is a competitive advantage.
Kerry Huang’s framing applies directly here. Architecture creates the conditions where the choice to ignore is no longer invisible. Readiness Infrastructure is the board-level category name for that concept.
Buyer Group Three. Mid-Market IT and Operations Leaders. Wayne’s Audience.
This is the SMB and mid-market buyer who will hear about Readiness OS on Wayne’s podcast on May 14. Business owners and IT leaders inside the Microsoft ecosystem.
This group hears category language differently from Fortune 1000 executives. They respond to plain language that describes a problem they recognize from their own experience. Readiness Infrastructure is slightly abstract for this buyer without context. What resonates with Wayne’s audience is the specific pain. You have Microsoft’s full AI stack. You do not have the operating model that lets you use it when something real happens.
For this group the category name matters less than the scenario. Ransomware at 3am. Regulatory notice with a 48 hour window. Key customer departure. What do you do in the first 30 minutes. If the answer is figure out who owns it you need Readiness Infrastructure.
The market resonance verdict
Readiness Infrastructure is the right category name for two of your three buyer groups. Fortune 1000 C-suite and boards will hear it and understand it immediately. The mid-market buyer needs the scenario first and the category name second.
The category framing that works across all three groups is not just the name. It is the name plus the one-line definition that follows it.
Readiness Infrastructure. The preparation architecture that makes organizations fearless in the face of any strategic trigger. Built before the trigger fires. Deployed in 12 minutes when it does.
That three-sentence definition works for the Fortune 1000 CIO, the board member approving the budget, and the mid-market business owner who just lived through a ransomware scare.
