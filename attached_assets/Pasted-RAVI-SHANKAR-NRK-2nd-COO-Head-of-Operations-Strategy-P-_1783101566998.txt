Two signatures. Both valid. The system still failed.
Six weeks after a hospital delegation was properly re-attested — Structural Change Trigger fired, both signatures produced — a routine retraining pull executed under conditions nobody had attested to. Nothing in the architecture failed. It just never had a clock for that gap.
That gap is what "Three Clocks" is about.
It started as one precise question from a reader: do the two triggers fire at delegation time, or execution time? The honest answer was neither — and answering it exposed a third clock the whole two-signature model had been silently assuming away.
What the thread produced next: two competing candidates for what runs on that clock (a deterministic verifier vs. a first-principles admissibility law), a public concession from a vendor about his own tool's ceiling, a seven-link chain that places all three clocks on one ordered route, and a readiness question exposing that the "legitimacy" signature has been certifying intent while quietly ignoring capability.
A signature is a statement about the moment it was signed. Execution is always later. No number of signatures closes that distance. Only a third clock does.
