The frame
Must-have on first engagement is not built from more features. It is built from three things: the executive sees themselves on screen, they feel the stakes, and they leave believing the machine is doing work they cannot do themselves. Every recommendation below is judged against that bar.
What to add
Role and industry capture at first touch. This is the single highest-leverage change in the entire product. The second an executive lands — before Mission Control, before any playbook — capture two fields: role (CEO, CFO, COO, CIO, CHRO, CLO, Board) and industry. Then dynamically tailor every playbook example, every dollar figure, every stakeholder name, every trigger scenario to their world. A healthcare CFO should see healthcare-CFO triggers with realistic healthcare financial exposure numbers. Right now every executive sees the same generic library. That is a must-have killer. Fix this and the whole product feels like it was built for them specifically.
“Your Board’s Worst Tuesday” generator. One page. Type your company name. The system pulls public data (10-K, recent news, industry) and generates the three most probable trigger scenarios against that specific business over the next 12 months, with the pre-staged playbook for each. This is visceral, personal, shareable, and it travels from executive to executive faster than any content you can post on LinkedIn. This replaces the generic Executive Brief and extends the Executive Departure Brief pattern — which is already the best asset in the platform.
The counter-factual timeline. On every activation debrief, show what would have happened without the playbook. Hour-by-hour. “At 07:15 without Readiness OS, your general counsel would still be drafting the initial memo. At 11:40, first board notification. At day 14, regulatory disclosure deadline passes.” This is how executives justify a $250K ACV to their board. The ROI Dashboard shows value preserved. The counter-factual shows value at risk. You need both.
Peer benchmark pulse. “73 other Fortune 1000 CFOs have activated this playbook in the last 30 days.” Executives move when peers move. This is the most powerful enterprise signal there is. You cannot show it today because you have no customers. Simulate it honestly in the interim — “Based on RSS signal volume across 248 sources, this trigger category fired 17 times last week across the Fortune 1000.” Directional truth, not fabricated.
A board-packet export that looks like McKinsey prepared it. From any activation, one click produces a PDF-perfect board packet with their logo. You have the ROI board report today. Elevate it to something an executive wants to generate on purpose. The packet becomes the reason they activate — which means they use the product weekly, not monthly.
What to cut or minimize
Too many front doors. You have TryDemo, GuidedStart, DemoRouter, Executive Departure Brief, industry demos hub, four individual industry demos, DemoAccess with tokens, OneClickDemo redirected, TestDrive, TwelveMinuteTestDrive. That is ten public entry points. An executive confronting that many choices bounces. Pick one — I would build from the Executive Departure Brief pattern — and kill or hide the rest. Consolidation is not a cost. It is a gift to the buyer.
151 pages. You have roughly three times the surface area of what a Fortune 1000 pilot actually needs. Every page outside the critical path from “aha” to “pilot” to “renewal” dilutes attention and creates maintenance load for a solo build. I would audit mercilessly and cut 80 pages. The duplicate intelligence hubs alone (SignalIntelligenceHub, IntelligenceHub, IntelligenceControlCenter, ComprehensiveAIIntelligence, McKinseyIntelligenceCenter) should collapse to one.
The 5-step onboarding wizard. The activation guide we just wrote correctly tells users to skip it. If the guide says skip, the wizard is the wrong shape. Replace it entirely with a 60-second role+industry capture inside Mission Control. No wizard. No “Begin Phase 1.” Just identification and then the pre-staged trigger already pulsing.
“Free sample playbooks” as a concept. Three public, 167 locked is freemium thinking. Fortune 1000 executives do not care about samples. They care about relevance. Replace with: the moment they enter role + industry, show the seven most critical playbooks for their situation — all fully viewable, all unlocked. The gate moves from “3 vs 167” to “you can view, but you cannot activate without a pilot.”
The Growth Segment public presence. Until one Fortune 1000 pilot closes, the SMB tier dilutes the enterprise positioning. Keep the code. Bury the page. No nav entry, no footer link. Serve it to SMB inbound that asks. Your enterprise story needs to be the only story for the next 60 days.
Football terminology in URL routes and DB enums. User-facing labels were retired — good. But URLs still resolve against offense and special_teams. Procurement teams inspect these. Fix before the first pilot kicks off IT review.
What to leave alone
The Executive Departure Brief. The 3,600× framing and 30-day baseline. The VaughnMartin and Readiness OS naming. The Origin prologue in the Founder Story. The brand color system. The Microsoft positioning. The PlaybookActivationConsole with auto-task seeding. The magic link plus welcome trigger email — that is quietly brilliant.
The three I would do first
If you can only do three in the next 30 days before the first pilot: role and industry capture at first touch, consolidation to one primary demo entry point, and the counter-factual timeline on every debrief. Those three move the product from “impressive enterprise SaaS” to “I cannot run my function without this.“​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​
