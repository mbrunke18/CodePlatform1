This week, AwaCourage spoke to me in Japanese 🇯🇵

My Forbes article was just translated and published by Forbes Japan.

And that signal told me something I hadn’t fully seen before.

The challenges we face in supply chains are not local — they are structural, global, and shared.

Across industries and countries, we continue to see the same pattern:
Technology investment alone does not create competitive advantage.

From studying 408 firms across manufacturing industries, one insight stands out — and it is uncomfortable for most boardrooms:

Technology adoption alone has zero statistical relationship with supply chain collaboration improvement.

Zero. Not weak. Not marginal. Zero.

The business case most companies present to their boards simply does not exist in the data.

Technology doesn’t build a moat.
Capability and governance do.

The real gap is not digital — it is organizational.

Lidl spent seven years and nearly $580 million on a world-class SAP system.
They walked away from it in 2018.

Siemens Amberg built one of the world’s most advanced smart factories —
not by installing technology first,
but by ensuring operators had real decision rights before the system arrived.

Same investment category. Opposite results.

It was who owned the decision before it arrived.

Companies don’t fail because they lack systems.
They fail because they outsource judgment before building it.

This is where I believe a new kind of leadership is required — what I call AwaCourage.

“Awareness without AwaCourage is merely a well-lit prison cell.”

Because in the end,
AI will scale decisions —
but only organizations can build the capability to make them right.

Thank you to Forbes Japan for bringing this conversation to Japanese readers.

The problem has no borders. The conversation shouldn’t either.

Three thinkers whose conversations have sharpened how I see this problem:
Richard Goyette Grant Oliff Oscar Chavarria 
Grateful for the ongoing exchange.

Where in your organization does the decision live — and who actually owns it when the system fails?

Martin, “the capability compounds, the moat deepens” is the formulation that matters most to me here, because it names something the 408-firm data kept pointing at without landing on: the organizations where capability held longest weren’t just better prepared, they had built a feedback mechanism that made each deployment more accurate than the last. The debrief encoding what worked back into preparation isn’t just a quality improvement loop — it’s the mechanism by which the moat becomes genuinely harder to replicate, because the competitor can buy the platform but can’t buy the accumulated decision logic embedded in the preparation phase.

What I’m still working out is the failure mode at the seam between preparation and deployment. In your system — where decision rights, ownership, and response architecture are all established during low pressure before any trigger fires — what’s the earliest signal that the preparation phase didn’t actually transfer when real pressure arrived? Not that the playbook failed, but that the organization reverted to improvising rather than deploying what it had already decided.
