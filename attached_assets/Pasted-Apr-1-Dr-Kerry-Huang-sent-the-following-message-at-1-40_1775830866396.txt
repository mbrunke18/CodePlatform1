This week, AwaCourage spoke to me in Japanese 🇯🇵

My Forbes article was just translated and published by Forbes Japan.

And that signal told me something I hadn’t fully seen before.

The challenges we face in supply chains are not local — they are structural, global, and shared.

Across industries and countries, we continue to see the same pattern:
Technology investment alone does not create competitive advantage.

From studying 408 firms across manufacturing industries, one insight stands out — and it is uncomfortable for most boardrooms:

Technology adoption alone has zero statistical relationship with supply chain collaboration improvement.

Zero. Not weak. Not marginal. Zero.

The business case most companies present to their boards simply does not exist in the data.

Technology doesn’t build a moat.
Capability and governance do.

The real gap is not digital — it is organizational.

Lidl spent seven years and nearly $580 million on a world-class SAP system.
They walked away from it in 2018.

Siemens Amberg built one of the world’s most advanced smart factories —
not by installing technology first,
but by ensuring operators had real decision rights before the system arrived.

Same investment category. Opposite results.

It was who owned the decision before it arrived.

Companies don’t fail because they lack systems.
They fail because they outsource judgment before building it.

This is where I believe a new kind of leadership is required — what I call AwaCourage.

“Awareness without AwaCourage is merely a well-lit prison cell.”

Because in the end,
AI will scale decisions —
but only organizations can build the capability to make them right.

Thank you to Forbes Japan for bringing this conversation to Japanese readers.

The problem has no borders. The conversation shouldn’t either.

Three thinkers whose conversations have sharpened how I see this problem:
Richard Goyette Grant Oliff Oscar Chavarria 
Grateful for the ongoing exchange.

Where in your organization does the decision live — and who actually owns it when the system fails?

Dr Huang, 
The failure mode at the seam shows up before the debrief and before any outcome is visible. The earliest signal is not that the playbook failed. It is whether the person who owned the decision during preparation steps into it under pressure or steps back from it.

When a trigger fires every owner has a pre-assigned acknowledgment step. That step is visible in real time. Silence at acknowledgment is the signal. Not silence at completion. Not divergence at debrief. Silence at the moment the response either deploys or does not. That surfaces within the first minutes of execution before anyone can assess whether the playbook worked.

What we have found is that the organizations that hold at the seam are not the ones with the best documentation. They are the ones who built ownership during preparation rather than assignment. Those are different things. Assignment is naming someone. Ownership is when that person has challenged the playbook, rehearsed the decision, and signed off on it before any pressure existed. When the trigger fires they are not reading a document. They are executing a decision they already made.

The debrief captures the second signal. If the organization improvised rather than deployed the debrief encodes why and that encoding feeds back into better preparation for the next deployment. But by then the window has already closed. The first signal is always acknowledgment.

Your concept of AwaCourage is the right frame for that distinction. The courage to act on what was already decided rather than retreating to the comfort of real-time deliberation under pressure.

Martin Brunke   10:11 AM
Dr Huang,

The question cuts to the most important operational distinction I have seen across twenty years.

Yes the acknowledgment step can be gamed. And the tell is not in the first minutes. It is in mid-execution behavior exactly as you described. Someone can perform ownership at acknowledgment and revert under sustained pressure because what they built during preparation was familiarity not commitment.

The deepest foundation of this product comes from five years coaching major college football at Stanford University. The programs that held under pressure were not the ones with better game plans. They were the ones where every player had been in the room when the play was built, had the right to challenge it during the week, and had rehearsed their specific decision until it was an artifact of preparation not a performance of acknowledgment. The quarterback who helped construct the play on Tuesday, challenged the blocking scheme on Wednesday, and ran the scenario against the scout team on Thursday owns a decision his body knows before his mind processes it. The player who watched film and acknowledged the scheme on Friday performs ownership on the walk-through Saturday morning and reverts when the defense shows a look nobody prepared for.

That distinction is what I carried from the field into twenty years of Fortune 1000 operations. And it is the foundation of how the preparation phase was designed.

The preparation that built ownership that held did three things differently. It put the owner in the room when the response was being built not when it was being delivered. It gave the owner explicit challenge rights before the playbook was final. And it was personalized to the specific decision this specific person would face under their specific conditions not a generic response assigned to a role.

The acknowledgment step is a reliable test for ownership built the first way. It is not a reliable test for ownership built the second way. The platform was designed to build the first kind. Whether it always does depends on how the preparation phase was run.
