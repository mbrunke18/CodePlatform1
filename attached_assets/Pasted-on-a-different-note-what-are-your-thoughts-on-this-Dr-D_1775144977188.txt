Every CEO wears a lens that they don't know is costing them every day – in the decisions they make, the hires they agree, the strategy they develop and the growth they deliver.
It's not a physical lens. It's not even one anyone can see.
It's neurological, shaped by decades of experience, learning, pressure, and consequence, and drives everything a CEO needs to consider.
It decides what gets noticed in meetings, what is viewed as urgent, what gets dismissed before it's fully considered.
It decides what attention is brought to every single decision, of every single day.
The lens is a natural default the brain uses to manage an impossible volume of information coming at it every second. But the lens is invisible to the person wearing it.
Until it's made visible — and then their view of the world, business and life changes.
The market just confirmed what I've been building toward
Recently, one of the world's largest executive search firms publicly launched an AI-enabled leadership assessment platform built around how leaders think and decide under pressure. They called it the "decision imprint."
This is the market now noticing why it's so important to focus on decision making, and not personality testing, mindset or other past trends in the leadership market space.
This is not about just making decisions. It's about how the brain changes when faced with pressure and in particular sustained pressure.
How does a leader decide and not just what have they decided. That's the question that matters for business, for growth, for movement, for staying ahead.
The complex environments of today demand a different approach.
But here's where the gap still remains.
What's been built observes decision behaviour under simulated conditions — a leader can see what happens and what they decide when faced with pressure. This is valuable. But it's still measuring the output of the lens, not the lens itself.
And yet it's exactly the lens that decides what information reaches the leader's awareness before a decision is ever made.
So, in effect, a leader without knowledge of their lens is using a screened version of information to make decisions.
Think of it like choosing from a menu where half the options are hidden. You make the best decision available but not necessarily the best decision possible.
This is not something visible in simulation. It requires a different kind of measurement entirely.
And the cost of leaving it unmeasured is the difference between firms that will excel and those that will stagnate. This is where EBITDA erodes. Where M&A decisions drift. Where succession risk compounds. Where the wrong hire gets made because nobody noticed what wasn't being discussed.
The people sitting around the CEO's table
The most expensive misalignment in any organisation isn't between the CEO and the board. It's between the people sitting around the CEO's table.
Each one sees a different version of the same problem, because each person's lens, built over decades, determines what information makes it through to their awareness even before the conversation starts.
What feels urgent to one person barely registers for another. This isn't just about roles: it's about the screened information each person sees within as well as across their own role.
Decision frameworks are a sound investment, until pressure hits and filtered information creates dissonance rather than clarity, exactly when it's needed most.
The good news is, the lens is measurable.
Once a leadership team understands each other's lenses, this creates growth through differences rather than stagnation through conflict. Each person starts to understand what others are likely to see, but importantly what they're likely to miss.
That's when a leadership team becomes genuinely greater than the sum of its parts.
The commercial cost of invisible lenses
McKinsey research shows that 58% of executive decision time is used ineffectively, which translates to $250 million annually for a Fortune 500 company. Cambridge University research shows 60% of executives experience impaired judgment after prolonged decision-making. 72% of senior executives say bad strategic decisions are as frequent as good ones.
The stats are interesting. But they hide the true problem: the unexamined lens.
What's being built
I've spent 26 years studying how people make decisions under pressure in the highest-stakes environments. What I've learned underpins a diagnostic I built that measures the lens — not just what a leader does, but what information their neurological system shows them before they decide.
The A³ framework maps three layers beneath every decision: where attention goes under pressure, what information the leader is missing when making decisions, and why their brain built that pattern in the first place.
The output is a Decision Signature that is individual, specific, evidenced, and with an actionable plan for change. This is not to be mistaken with a personality profile, coaching, or mindset work. It is none of those. It is a neurological understanding of how information gets filtered under pressure.
It's the difference between assessing a car in the garage compared to the same car operating at speed on the motorway.
The same methodology maps leadership teams, showing how each person's lens interacts with others, where conflicts can arise, and what that means for every decision the team makes collectively.
One question to close with
What if the most expensive risk in your portfolio isn't in the numbers? But rather in the lens of those reading them?
Uncovering your lens is where growth, precision, and clarity begin.
